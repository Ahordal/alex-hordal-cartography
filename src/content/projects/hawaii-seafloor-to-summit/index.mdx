Measured from its base on the abyssal seafloor, Mauna Kea rises roughly 9,743 metres (31,965 feet), making it the tallest mountain on Earth. Hawaiʻi: Seafloor to Summit is a 48 × 36 inch topobathymetric map showing the full vertical extent of the eight main Hawaiian Islands, from the abyssal plain to the summit of Mauna Kea.

Conventional topographic maps measure elevation from sea level, which hides most of each Hawaiian volcano beneath the ocean. This map shows land and seafloor as one continuous surface.

The topobathymetric surface is a mosaic of three DEM sources, layered in order of detail. The NOAA NCEI Coastal Relief Model (CRM, ~30 m) covers the islands and nearshore seafloor. Multibeam bathymetry from SOEST’s Hawaiʻi Mapping Research Group (~50 m) adds higher-resolution detail farther offshore where surveys exist. ETOPO2022 (~450 m) fills the remaining gaps.

Empty edge cells in the CRM, the grey areas in the following image, were not transparent but stored as 0 m, so they were set to NoData. All three sources were then projected to a common grid in Hawaii Albers Equal Area Conic (ESRI:102007), which preserves area and keeps distortion low across the main Hawaiian Islands, and merged with a blended mosaic, so each fades into the next rather than meeting at a hard seam. A 30 m grid was tested first, but 90 m kept the file manageable and hid faint alignment artifacts along the joins, with no loss of detail visible at the scale of the full island chain.

The merged DEM is symbolized with a custom diverging colour ramp that blends topography and bathymetry at sea level. Colour stops, positions, and transparencies were adjusted over many iterations. The DEM was classified using a defined interval of 100 m, producing 104 classes across the full elevation range. Terrain shading comes from a single hillshade (315° azimuth, 45° altitude) run on the merged DEM, so no shading lines form along the dataset boundaries. It sits over the colour ramp with a Multiply blend mode and uses a z-factor of 1.5 to bring out the broad, low-relief landforms on the seafloor.

Bathymetric and terrestrial features were identified and labelled, including points of interest, population centres, summits, volcanoes, seamounts, and underwater landslides. All type is set in Source Sans Pro, and within that single family, typography doubles as a depth cue: land, channel, and bay labels are crisp and bright, while seafloor features are set in deliberately faint, letterspaced blue type that recedes into the water. The bright Pacific Ocean label is placed over the darker Hawaiian Arch label beneath it, so the two read as sitting at different depths. Together, these contrasts make the terrain feel more three-dimensional. Hawaiian place names use the ʻokina and kahakō.

The map also includes several small contextual snippets, from the islands’
earlier name, the Sandwich Islands, to a personal note marking my engagement on
the Nā Pali Coast, along with an inset map of the Hawaiian
Archipelago for location context and a seafloor-to-summit elevation profile
of Mauna Kea.

The elevation profile uses a vertical exaggeration of 8.5× to accentuate
Mauna Kea’s broad, low-angle slopes. It was also partly a practical choice:
8.5× let the profile fit the page composition, matching the width of the inset
archipelago map.

The final composition was completed in Adobe Illustrator, including finalizing
text choices, cartographic fixes and refinements, and the addition of contextual
information. The border is deliberately raised, with bevelled edges, to
reinforce the map’s sense of elevation and depth.
