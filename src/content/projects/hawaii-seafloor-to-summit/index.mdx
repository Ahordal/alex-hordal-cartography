Measured from its base on the abyssal seafloor, Mauna Kea rises roughly 9,743 metres (31,965 feet), making it the tallest mountain on Earth. Most of that height is underwater. Hawaiʻi: Seafloor to Summit is a 48 × 36 inch topobathymetric map showing the full vertical extent of the eight main Hawaiian Islands, from the abyssal plain to the summit of Mauna Kea.

Conventional topographic maps measure elevation from sea level, which hides most of each Hawaiian volcano beneath the ocean. This map shows land and seafloor as one continuous surface.

The topobathymetric surface is a mosaic of three DEM sources, layered in order of detail. The NOAA NCEI Coastal Relief Model (CRM, ~30 m) covers the islands and nearshore seafloor. Multibeam bathymetry from SOEST’s Hawaiʻi Mapping Research Group (~50 m) adds higher-resolution detail farther offshore where surveys exist. ETOPO2022 (~450 m) fills the remaining gaps. All three were resampled to a common 90 m grid to balance detail, continuity, and visual consistency across the surface. The sources reference different vertical datums, but the resulting offsets of a few metres are negligible across an elevation range of nearly 10,000 metres.

A key design consideration was the symbolization of the merged DEM using a custom, continuous, diverging colour ramp that seamlessly blended topography and bathymetry at mean sea level. This involved extensive experimentation with colour stops, positioning, transparencies, and manual classification intervals. Hillshading and slope highlights were applied to accentuate geomorphology and improve terrain interpretation, particularly below the ocean surface.

Bathymetric and terrestrial features were identified and labelled, including points of interest, population centres, summits, volcanoes, seamounts, and underwater landslides. Typography doubles as a depth cue: land labels are crisp and bright, while undersea features are set in softer, wide-spaced blue type that recedes into the water, accentuating the pseudo-3D terrain effect. Hawaiian place names use the ʻokina and kahakō throughout.

The map also includes several small contextual snippets highlighting major features and important historical events, an inset map of the Hawaiian Archipelago for location context, and an elevation profile of Mauna Kea from abyssal plain to summit. A vertical exaggeration of 8.5× was used to accentuate Mauna Kea’s broad, low-angle slopes.
